What this document claims, in one paragraph

Couro takes a single phone video, runs a pose-estimation pipeline, reconstructs joint angles in 3D, predicts range of motion (ROM) per trial, and outputs sport-specific scoring. We measured the ROM-prediction layer (Layer 3, the slot that ships to production) against gold-standard motion capture using the standard biomechanics validity criteria: Lin's Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) > 0.60 and Bland-Altman 95% limits of agreement (LoA) within ±10°. By those criteria, 3 deploy slots are validated today, with a headline of CCC = 0.94, mean bias +1.3°, 95% LoA [−8.5°, +11.1°], MAE 4.4° for hip-adduction range-of-motion on the side-left camera across 12 leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) held-out subjects, 105 trials. Nine more slots clear weaker thresholds and are usable with caveats. The remaining slots are unvalidated and we explicitly identify which ones, why, and what we're doing about them.

What "validated" means here

Three statistical measures, all standard in the biomechanics literature, all measured per slot (where a slot is one metric × one camera angle):

Pearson r — correlation between Couro's prediction and mocap ground truth. Captures whether the two move in lockstep. Sensitive to noise but blind to systematic offset and scale mismatch. A model that consistently overshoots ROM by 4° still gets r = 1.0 if it tracks the shape correctly.

Lin's Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) — Pearson r penalized for bias and scale mismatch. The standard biomechanics validity statistic. CCC > 0.60 is the literature floor for "validated against gold standard"; > 0.80 is "excellent." Reference: Lin 1989, McBride 2005, Atkinson & Nevill 1998.

Bland-Altman 95% limits of agreement (LoA) — the range within which 95% of the (prediction − ground truth) differences fall. Reports in degrees. ±10° is the clinical-utility bar for goniometric measurement; ±5° is the inter-rater agreement floor for two human raters using a hand goniometer. Reference: Bland & Altman 1986, Hancock 2018.

Why CCC matters more than Pearson r for product validation. A pose system can score r = 0.95 on a metric and still be unfit for deployment if it has a systematic 4° overshoot at the population level. CCC catches that; Pearson r doesn't. For a sport-scoring product where athletes are compared to clinical norms, scale calibration is part of correctness. v1 of this document led with r; v2 leads with CCC.

How Couro reads movement from video — the pipeline in plain English

Couro's pipeline runs in four stages. Each stage has its own accuracy measurement against mocap. The numbers above refer to Layer 3 (ROM prediction, the slot that ships).

Stage 1 — Keypoints

Find body landmarks in each video frame

A keypoint is a single (x, y) pixel location of a body landmark — like a dot on a stick-figure drawing. Couro detects 26 keypoints per frame using DWPose-L (ICCV 2023): nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, big toes, small toes, heels, head_top, neck, hip_center.

Plain English: "The right knee is at pixel (480, 720) in this video frame."

Layer 1 measurement: pixel-level Pearson r against mocap-derived projected keypoints = 0.98 (front 0.989, side 0.985, rear 0.983). This is a measurement of the keypoint detector, not the joint-angle pipeline downstream.

Stage 2 — Joint angles

Compute the angle between three keypoints in 3D

A joint angle is the angle formed by three keypoints — e.g. knee flexion = angle between the hip→knee vector and the knee→ankle vector. We reconstruct this in 3D from 2D keypoints using anthropometric scaling (your femur is ~25% of your height).

Plain English: "The right knee was flexed to 85° at the moment of landing."

Layer 2 measurement: per-frame angle reading vs mocap, pooled across all metrics × all views = |r| ≈ 0.51. This is the raw angle-reconstruction layer, before any regression cleanup. Layer 3 below sits on top.

Stage 3 — Range of motion (ROM)

Summarize the trial as peak-to-peak movement

ROM = peak angle minus minimum angle during the trial. One number per joint per trial. Per-(metric × camera angle) ridge regression on top of Layer 2 angle traces, with LOSO cross-validation.

Plain English: "Your knee moved through 65° of motion during this drop jump."

Layer 3 is the model that ships in product. This is the layer measured against the CCC + Bland-Altman validity criteria in this document.

Stage 4 — Sport scoring

Compare measured ROM to sport-specific population norms

Sport configuration files define target ranges for each metric based on clinical-population means. Couro's score for an athlete is the distance of their measurements from those targets, surfaced as risk flags and ranking.

Plain English: "Your knee valgus of 9° during landing exceeds the ACL-risk threshold of 5°."

Layer 4 is a calibration question (are the targets right?), not a measurement question. Discussed in its own section below.

Per-trial vs per-subject divergence — why v1's "deploy-ready" table was misleading

v1 of this document reported Layer 3 accuracy using per-trial Pearson r — treating each individual jump as an independent observation. The current analysis uses per-subject aggregation (one mean prediction and one mean ground-truth value per subject), which is the standard biomechanics approach because individual trials within a subject are correlated and aren't statistically independent.

On most slots, the two agree. On the front_center camera slots for hip flexion and knee, they diverge severely:

Slot Per-trial r (what v1 reported) Per-subject r (actual validity) Δ Interpretation
Hip flexion R · front_center +0.78 −0.21 0.99 Model tracks within-subject rep variation but anti-ranks athletes
Knee R · front_center +0.70 +0.22 0.48 Per-trial signal is dominated by within-subject noise, not athlete differences
What this means. A negative per-subject r doesn't mean the model is broken at the algorithm level — it means the model picks up rep-to-rep variation within a subject (real signal) but fails to rank one subject's ROM higher than another (the actual product use case). For a tool whose purpose is comparing athletes against population norms, that's a deployment-blocking failure. These two front_center slots should be demoted from the deploy_ready_models.json before they ship in product.

Three other slots show smaller per-trial vs per-subject divergence (Δ > 0.15) and warrant a closer look before shipping: hip adduction front_center (Δ 0.29), hip adduction front_oblique_right (Δ 0.34), knee front_oblique_left (Δ 0.14). All have positive but reduced per-subject CCC.

Layer 4 — sport scoring configuration calibration

Calibration (not measurement)

Scoring targets are defined against published clinical norms

Couro's sport-specific scoring configurations (freeflow, track, treadmill, basketball, softball) define target ranges for each metric. Those targets are set against population means from peer-reviewed mocap studies — they are not measurements of Couro's product accuracy. This is a calibration claim, not a measurement claim.

Calibration sources: Fukuchi RBDS (28 runners), DVJ Basketball (20), ACL Jump-Landing (42), GaitRec (211 healthy gait), OpenCap LabValidation (9). Process: ~2,000 autoresearch optimization experiments selecting target values that minimize z-distance from clinical population means while preserving sport-specific discrimination.

What v1 got wrong here, corrected. v1 reported "92–95% composite alignment" and "0.13σ z-error" as if these were measurement-accuracy numbers. They are optimization objective values from the calibration-target-selection process. They describe how well the chosen targets align with population norms, not how well Couro's measurements align with those targets. The latter is the Layer 3 validation above. v2 separates these two questions.

License disclosure on the preliminary numbers. The 8-subject rear-view cohort uses MPI-INF-3DHP (Mehta et al., 3DV 2017) under its academic-only license.
