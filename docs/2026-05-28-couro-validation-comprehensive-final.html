The Couro validation story in one paragraph

Couro takes a video from a single phone, runs a pose-estimation model, reconstructs the athlete's joint angles in 3D, and outputs sport-specific biomechanical metrics and risk scores. We have measured the accuracy of every layer of this pipeline against gold-standard motion capture across 582+ athletes from 8 independent peer-reviewed academic datasets. The result splits into two trust points: (1) the pose detector finds the body with sub-centimeter accuracy and r ≥ 0.98 lockstep with mocap across front, side, and rear angles; (2) the sport scoring configurations are calibrated to within 0.13σ of population norms from clinical mocap datasets, across 42 validated metrics. From one phone, no calibration.

How Couro reads movement from video — the pipeline in plain English

Before showing accuracy numbers, here's what they actually measure. Couro's pipeline runs four stages, and accuracy at each stage enables different downstream capabilities. The two big questions to keep straight: "where is the body?" (keypoints) vs "what is the body doing?" (joint angles, ROM, scoring).

Stage 1 — Keypoints

Find body landmarks in each video frame

A keypoint is a single (x, y) point on the body in pixel coordinates — like a dot on a stick-figure drawing. Couro detects 26 keypoints per frame: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, big toes, small toes, heels, head_top, neck, hip_center.

Plain-English example: "The right knee is at pixel (480, 720) in this video frame."

What accuracy at this stage enables:

  • Visual playback with skeleton overlay on the athlete's video
  • Body-part timing measurements (when did the foot strike happen?)
  • 2D body-side comparisons
  • EVERYTHING downstream depends on this layer being accurate.
Stage 2 — Joint angles

Compute the angle between three keypoints

A joint angle is the angle formed by three keypoints. Example: knee flexion = angle between the hip→knee vector and the knee→ankle vector. We compute this in 3D after estimating body dimensions from keypoints (anthropometric scaling).

Plain-English example: "The right knee was flexed to 85° at the moment of landing."

What accuracy at this stage enables:

  • Movement profiles (how did your knee angle change over the whole jump?)
  • Form analysis (did your trunk lean too far forward?)
  • Comparison to clinical norms (was peak knee flexion in the healthy range?)
  • This is what makes the system clinically meaningful — angles are how PTs and biomech researchers describe movement.
Stage 3 — Range of motion (ROM)

Summarize the trial as peak-to-peak movement

ROM = peak angle minus minimum angle during the trial. A single number per joint per trial.

Plain-English example: "Your knee moved through 65° of motion during this drop jump (10° standing → 75° landing)."

What accuracy at this stage enables:

  • Trial-to-trial progress tracking ("you increased your hip ROM by 8° in 4 weeks")
  • Sport-specific performance metrics (sprint stride length, pitching arm path)
  • Risk thresholds ("hip flex ROM < 90° during squat increases injury risk")
  • This is what the v14 ridge regression models predict directly.
Stage 4 — Sport scoring

Interpret what the movement means for sport, risk, performance

Compare measured joint angles, ROM, and timing to sport-specific population norms. Flag risk, surface insights, rank performance.

Plain-English example: "Your knee valgus of 9° during landing exceeds the ACL injury risk threshold of 5° — flagged as moderate risk."

What accuracy at this stage enables:

  • "Good vs bad" assessment of an athlete's movement
  • Injury risk flags (ACL, ITB, runner's knee, shoulder)
  • Return-to-play decisions after injury
  • Training program adjustments based on biomechanical findings
  • This is what the athlete and coach actually see in the app.
The most important distinction: "Keypoints" = WHERE the body is in the image (dots). "Joint angles" = HOW the body is bent in 3D space (angles between three dots). Keypoint accuracy is necessary but not sufficient for joint angle accuracy — even with perfect 2D dots, the conversion to 3D angles introduces measurement noise. That's why our keypoint r is 0.98 but our joint-angle r is 0.40-0.74. Both numbers are honest measurements of different things, and both matter.

Why "great keypoints" doesn't automatically mean "great joint angles"

This is a real and important nuance. The accuracy of Couro's keypoint detection is 5-8 mm with r=0.98 — essentially as good as gold-standard mocap markers. But the joint angle r drops to 0.40-0.74. Why?

Because a joint angle requires 3D information that a single 2D camera can't fully see.

What's hard Why How Couro handles it
Depth ambiguity A knee bending toward the camera looks similar to a knee just being closer to the camera. From a single 2D image, you can't always tell. We use anthropometric scaling (your femur is ~25% of your height) to estimate the 3D position from the 2D keypoint plus your body proportions.
Out-of-plane motion A 90° knee bend seen from directly the side looks like 90° on screen. The same bend seen from the front looks like 0° (the legs just look shorter). The per-(metric × camera angle) ridge regression at Layer 3 learns which angles are observable from which views. That's why our deploy table varies by angle.
Body proportions vary A 70" athlete and a 60" athlete with the same knee bend will produce different pixel measurements. We use body-aware scoring (anthropometric scaler) to normalize across body sizes. Reduces RMSE by ~10-15%.
Occlusion If your right knee passes behind your left leg, the detector loses it for a few frames. Temporal smoothing (Kalman + Butterworth, both autoresearch-optimized) fills in short occlusions. Confidence-aware filtering rejects long ones.
The honest tradeoff: Couro chose the "single phone, no calibration" constraint. That means we accept some loss going from 2D pixels to 3D angles. The accuracy we DO achieve (4.75° joint angle MAE, r=0.91 ROM on best slots) is comparable to 2-camera OpenCap and approaches 8-camera Theia3D on hip/knee flexion from oblique angles. The system is honest about which (metric × view) combinations work well and which don't — that's what the per-slot deploy table is for.

Couro's pipeline runs in four stages. Each stage has its own validation against motion capture. Trust point #1 covers Layers 1–3 (technical accuracy). Trust point #2 is Layer 4 (clinical scoring sense).

Pearson r per camera: front 0.989 · side 0.985 · rear 0.983 · cross-subject SD < 0.01. Strongest joints: neck, shoulders, head_top, pelvis (r ≥ 0.99). Weakest: right ankle (r=0.96, S6 outlier), toes. Worst single cell across 8 subjects × 18 keypoints: r=0.882.
Why r AND mm both matter: r captures whether keypoints move in lockstep with mocap (0.98 = essentially yes). MPJPE captures whether they land at the right pixel (10.3 px = yes within 5-8 mm). Both are real measurements, both required.
