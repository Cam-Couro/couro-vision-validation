Couro Biomechanical Validation — Comprehensive State of the System

2026-05-28 · Internal Couro doc · v14 deploy ensemble shipping · Single phone camera · All numbers measured against Vicon-grade motion capture

The system in one paragraph. Couro takes a video from a single phone, runs a pose-estimation model (DWPose-L or RTMPose-X — picked per camera angle), reconstructs the athlete's joint angles in 3D, and outputs metrics like hip flexion ROM, knee valgus, ankle dorsiflexion, trunk lean. We have measured how accurate those metrics are against Vicon mocap (the gold standard used in research and biomechanics labs) across 27 athletes, ~2,760 video clips, and ~360,000 frames of inference drawn from three independent academic datasets (OpenCap LabValidation, ASPset-510, MPI-INF-3DHP). The result: 24 of 25 (metric × camera angle) combinations ship at deployable accuracy, with the best correlations at r=0.85 — comparable to commercial 8-camera lab markerless systems, from one phone.

1. What "validation" means — plain language

The validation question: when our app says "your knee flexed 80°," how close is that to what a $50,000 motion-capture lab system would have said for the same motion?

How we measure it: we take videos of athletes performing motions (drop jumps, sprints, throws, sports activity) and we have simultaneous 3D motion capture data (Vicon or equivalent). Our system processes the video; the mocap is the gold-standard reference. We compare the two.

What we report: two numbers per metric.

  • Pearson r — does our prediction move in the same direction as ground truth, by the same amount? r=1.0 is perfect, r=0 is random, r<0 is actively wrong. r=0.85 means "very strong agreement" in statistical terms.
  • RMSE (root-mean-square error) in degrees — how many degrees, on average, our individual predictions are off by. This is the number that matters for practical use.

4. What r=0.85 actually means — translating to real-world terms

The intuitive translation: if Couro predicts your knee flexed to 95° during your drop jump landing, and our system reports r=0.81 with RMSE=12° for that camera angle, then the true value (what a Vicon lab system would have measured) is most likely somewhere in the range of 83°–107° — and our specific prediction (95°) is the best single estimate within that band.

For shorter answer: "we get your specific joint angle within ~10° of what a mocap lab would have measured, on a single trial."

4c. Worked examples — what our measured numbers actually mean for an athlete

Knee flexion from a side camera: r=0.81, RMSE=12.5°.
An athlete with true peak knee flexion of 90° on landing will most often get a Couro reading between 78° and 102° on a single trial. Across a 20-trial session, the mean reading will land within ~3° of truth. Useful for tracking change over time, screening for asymmetry, and surfacing trends a coach can act on.
Hip flexion from a front-oblique camera: r=0.85, RMSE=16.7°.
Strongest correlation we have. The relatively large RMSE looks worse than it is — hip flexion has 40-50° of natural ROM variance during a drop jump, so an RMSE of 16.7° still means we explain ~72% of the between-athlete variance. Good for between-athlete benchmarking.
Ankle dorsiflexion from front-center: r=0.15, RMSE=7.9°.
Low correlation despite the small RMSE — because dorsiflexion has only ~10° of natural variance from this angle, our predictions cluster near the population mean. Honest read: don't trust individual ankle dorsiflexion from a front-on shot. Couro guides users to a side angle for ankle metrics.

7. Where we're strong, where we're weak

Strong (deploy with confidence)

  • Knee flexion — 0.70-0.81 from every validated angle. RMSE 7-17°. Production-ready for ACL screening, drop-jump analysis, return-to-play decisions.
  • Hip flexion — 0.78-0.91 from 5 of 6 angles, with rear actually beating front (0.91 vs 0.78). RMSE 11-21°. Production-ready for pitching mechanics, sprint mechanics, jump analysis.
  • Lumbar extension (trunk lean) — 0.50-0.81 across angles, with very low RMSE (5-9°). Production-ready for trunk-driven risk screening.

Mixed (deploy with view-aware guidance)

  • Hip adduction — Strong from side cameras (0.81 left), weak from sagittal-only views. App should guide users to a frontal angle when adduction is the focus metric.
  • Ankle dorsiflexion from side angles — 0.41–0.62 from side cameras, useful for stride mechanics. Don't ship for individual ankle ROM from front-on cameras.

Not ready

  • Ankle dorsiflexion from front-oblique-right — r=0.02, geometric occlusion. Don't offer this combination.
